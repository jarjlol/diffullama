\section{Experiments and Discussion}
\label{sec:experiments}

\lead{Setup}
We use 200 GSM8K questions for development and 300 held-out test questions for the five-model comparison \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}. Each model draws $N=10$ candidates per question. The models are DiffuGPT-S, DiffuGPT-M, and DiffuLLaMA \citep{gong2025diffullama}, Dream 7B \citep{ye2025dream7b}, and the from-scratch control LLaDA \citep{nie2025llada}. The five-model comparison uses 64 denoising steps. DiffuGPT-S uses one worked example and 192 new tokens because the prompt with four worked examples exceeds its 512-token context limit. We score candidates with GPT-2, Qwen2.5-0.5B, and TinyLlama. We also use Qwen2.5-7B for Dream and LLaMA-2-7B for DiffuLLaMA. We compute layer-averaged linear CKA \citep{kornblith2019cka} on up to 2,000 WikiText-103 segments.

\lead{Accuracy across five models}
On the 300 held-out questions, majority vote reaches 73.3\% on Dream and 73.0\% on LLaDA (Table~\ref{tab:final}). It closes 61 to 69\% of the gap between the first solution and oracle accuracy for these two models, consistent with earlier findings on self-consistency \citep{wang2023selfconsistency}. The oracle reaches at most 14.7\% on the other three models. Even a rule that always finds a correct solution when one exists has little room to improve on those outputs.

\lead{Voting across denoising steps}
Taking the first solution at 32 steps improves accuracy over the first solution at 16 steps by 9.8 points on Dream and 19.2 on LLaDA. Voting at 16 steps changes accuracy by $+0.6$ and $-0.4$ points. On the 500 questions shared by the 16-, 32-, and 64-step runs, voting closes 4\%, 26\%, and 68\% of the possible gain on Dream and $-2$\%, 53\%, and 61\% on LLaDA (Table~\ref{tab:budget}). The negative share means voting scores below the first solution.

On the questions with a 256-step run, voting closes 69\% of the available gap at 64 steps and 53\% at 256 steps on Dream. The corresponding shares are 68\% and 40\% on LLaDA. Even at 256 steps, voting adds 10.3 and 6.8 points over the first solution. A smaller first-to-oracle gap therefore explains only part of the smaller voting gain. Each comparison uses the same questions across its budgets. The 256-step subset is smaller than the set used for 16, 32, and 64 steps.

\lead{One longer solution or ten shorter solutions}
On the same 244 Dream questions, one 256-step greedy solution scores 82.4\%, compared with 73.4\% for voting over ten 64-step solutions. On 199 matched LLaDA questions, one greedy solution scores 72.9\%, compared with 76.4\% for voting. The more accurate choice therefore differs by model.

Median greedy generation times are 16.5 seconds per question for Dream and 17.9 for LLaDA. Generating ten 64-step solutions takes a mean of 36.8 and 55.0 seconds, respectively, over 500 questions (Table~\ref{tab:budget}). The timings cover generation only, use different question sets, and come from shared GPUs.

At 256 steps, voting over ten sampled solutions improves on one greedy solution by 3.2 points on Dream ($p=0.24$). On LLaDA it improves by 8.0 points ($n=137$, $p=0.007$).

\lead{AR reranking}
At 64 steps on all 500 test questions, reranking reaches 48.8\% on Dream and 52.2\% on LLaDA. These scores exceed the first solution and fall 23.6 and 21.0 points below voting ($p<0.001$ for both). On matched questions, Dream's reranking deficit falls from 20.0 points at 64 steps to 8.6 at 256 (95\% confidence interval for reranking minus voting $[-14.1,-3.2]$, $p=0.002$). LLaDA's deficit falls from 25.5 points to 2.6 (95\% confidence interval $[-6.8,+1.6]$, $p=0.33$). Both comparisons use the same AR scoring rule at each budget.

Excluding text after the final answer raises Dream's accuracy under the summed Qwen2.5-7B score by 13.0 points (95\% confidence interval $[9.0,17.0]$). LLaDA's accuracy changes little. The comparison holds the generated solutions fixed. The measured change comes from which part of each solution the AR model scores.

\lead{Comparison with published accuracy}
With 256 steps and no random sampling (temperature 0), accuracy reaches 81.0\% on Dream with our four-example prompt and 73.0\% on LLaDA ($n=100$ each). The published scores are 77.2\% for Dream \citep{ye2025dream7b} and 70.3\% for LLaDA \citep{nie2025llada}. The first sample from the original run scored 41.0\% and 51.0\% on these questions. The earlier run used 64 steps and temperature 0.7. At 256 steps, the temperature-0.7 first sample is 7.0 points below greedy on Dream and 2.9 points above it on LLaDA (paired, $n=185$ and $137$). The step budget accounts for most of the observed gap.

\lead{Model similarity and reranking gains}
The original scores place models with more similar activations higher on the measure of gain from reranking (Table~\ref{tab:preservation}). Spearman's rank correlation is $+1.0$ for 3 scorers, $+0.8$ for 6, and $+0.4$ for 1 (exact $p\geq 0.083$). Scoring only through the final answer changes the share of the gap closed by Qwen2.5-7B on Dream from $-9.7$\% to $+17.7$\%. We cannot recompute the four-model correlation because the three models with low oracle accuracy lack corrected scores. The change on Dream undermines the original ordering. We also test whether activation similarity on individual Dream questions predicts when reranking will recover a correct answer that the first solution missed. On 500 questions, the area under the ROC curve is 0.469 (permutation $p=0.32$). The odds ratio per standard deviation of similarity is 0.94 (95\% CI $[0.75,1.17]$). The Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment accounts for testing multiple layers. After adjustment, no layer shows a statistically significant association.
