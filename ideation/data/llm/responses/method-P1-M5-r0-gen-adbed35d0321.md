**Method: Denoising‑Trajectory Stability as a Proxy for Representation Preservation and Its Relation to Optimal Compute Allocation in Diffusion Language Models**

---

### Rationale  
The core hypothesis is that a diffusion language model (DLM) that retains more of the autoregressive (AR) backbone’s representations will have a denoising process that is already well‑aligned with the AR prior. In that case, additional denoising steps yield diminishing returns, whereas exploiting the model’s inherent candidate diversity (more samples + lightweight AR reranking) is a more efficient way to close the remaining “accuracy headroom.”  

Existing approaches quantify representation preservation by measuring similarity between DLM and AR hidden states (CKA, SVCCA, linear probes) or between output distributions (KL‑divergence of logits). These metrics are static or only capture *average* alignment across the whole denoising trajectory.  

We propose a **different, dynamical‑systems‑oriented angle**: the *speed at which the denoising trajectory stabilizes* (i.e., how quickly successive denoising steps stop changing the model’s prediction of the clean sequence). If the DLM’s internal representations are AR‑like, the denoising updates will quickly converge to a stable AR‑consistent prediction, resulting in a short stabilization time‑constant. Conversely, poor representation preservation leads to a slowly evolving trajectory that requires many steps to reach a stable prediction.  

Thus, we compute a **trajectory‑stability metric (τ)** from the denoising process itself and test whether τ predicts the relative benefit of (a) more denoising steps versus (b) more samples + AR reranking. This approach does not rely on explicit similarity measures between DLM and AR activations or logits; instead, it derives a property of the DLM’s own inference dynamics that we hypothesize is directly shaped by how much AR knowledge it has retained.  

By linking τ to the marginal quality‑efficiency trade‑off (QET) gains of the two compute‑allocation strategies across multiple model families and step‑budget regimes, we obtain a principled, inference‑time diagnostic that tells practitioners where to spend their compute budget without needing extra training or costly probing pipelines.

| **3. Capturing the denoising trajectory (stability measurement)** | For each DLM, run the **native sampler at a high step budget** (T = 256 steps) on a **representative subset** of 100 prompts (stratified across difficulty and domain). <br>• At each denoising step *t* (1 … T) the model outputs a prediction of the clean sequence, **x̂₀⁽ᵗ⁾** (either as token‑ID logits or as the expected embedding). <br>• Keep only the logits (or embeddings) of the current step and the previous step; compute the **L2 distance** between them, averaged over token positions and prompts:  <br>  Δₜ = ‖logitsₜ − logitsₜ₋₁‖₂ / (seq_len × batch). <br>• Accumulate Δₜ on the fly; no need to store the full logits tensor. <br>• After the generation, fit an **exponential decay** model Δₜ ≈ A·exp(−t/τ) (least‑squares on log‑Δ) to obtain a **time‑constant τ** (lower τ → faster stabilization). <br>• As a robust backup, also compute the **area‑under‑the‑curve (AUC)** of Δₜ (∑ₜΔₜ) – a larger AUC indicates slower stabilization. <br>Implementation uses activation‑checkpointing so that intermediate hidden‑states never exceed GPU memory; the extra cost is only a few kilobytes per step for the running average. | Approx. 6 GPU‑hrs (the diffusion forward passes are already required for the downstream QET measurements; the online distance computation adds negligible overhead). |
| **4. Candidate generation for QET measurement** | For **each** DLM (all four) and **each** prompt in the full 300‑prompt pool: <br>– Run the native diffusion sampler at three step budgets: **Low = 16**, **Medium = 64**, **High = 256** steps. <br>– At each budget generate **N ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8}** independent samples (different seeds). <br>– Use fp16 + activation‑checkpointing; for DiffuLLaMA additionally test 4‑bit quantisation *only* for the generation phase (the trajectory‑stability measurement in Step 3 stays in full precision). <br>– Store the generated token sequences (as IDs) to avoid recomputation for scoring. | Rough estimate: 300 prompts × 4 (N) × 3 (step budgets) × 4 (models) = 14 400 generations. | **5. Lightweight AR reranking** | Load a tiny AR scorer (GPT‑2‑small, 124 M) in fp16. For each set of N candidates compute the **average negative log‑likelihood (NLL)** of the full sequence (including EOS). The candidate with the lowest NLL is selected as the “sampling + reranking” output. The scorer runs on a single CPU core; its latency is overlapped with GPU kernel launches via CUDA streams, so its overhead is negligible for the efficiency metric. | CPU‑only; < 0.5 GPU‑hr equivalent. |
| **6. Quality evaluation** | For every output (denoising‑only and sampling + reranking) compute three task‑specific scores: <br>• HumanEval pass@1 and pass@10 → averaged → **code score**. <br>• GSM8K exact‑match accuracy. <br>• SIQA/WinoGrande accuracy (averaged). <br>Normalise each raw score to \[0,1\] across **all** conditions (model × step × N × strategy). <br>Take the unweighted mean of the three normalised scores → **Quality** ∈ \[0,1\]. | CPU‑side: < 1 hr. |
| **7. Efficiency measurement & QET** | Measure **wall‑clock latency per generated token** (including scorer overhead for the reranking condition) using `torch.cuda.Event` timestamps, averaged over all prompts and repetitions. <br>Convert latency to **Estimated Compute**:  `EstCompute = latency_per_token × peak_FP16_throughput (≈ 140 TFLOPs)`. <br>Define **Quality‑Efficiency Trade‑off (QET) = Quality / EstCompute**. Higher QET → more quality per unit of compute. | GPU‑side timing; negligible overhead. |
| **8. Analysis pipeline** | 1. **Marginal QET gains**: <br> a. ΔQET_denoise = QET(high steps, N=1) − QET(low steps, N=1) (and low→medium, medium→high). <br> b. ΔQET_sample = QET(medium steps, N=8) − QET(medium steps, N=1) (and 1→4, 4→8). <br>2. **Stability‑Gain Relationship**: Compute **Spearman’s ρ** between each model’s **τ** (or AUC) and the two sets of marginal gains (ΔQET_denoise, ΔQET_sample). <br>3. **Interaction test**: Fit a **linear mixed‑effects model** <br> `QET ~ τ * step_budget * N + (1|model) + (1|prompt_difficulty)` <br> including **model_family** (GPT‑2 vs. LLaMA) as a fixed effect to test whether the slope of τ differs across families and step budgets. <br>4. **Bootstrap uncertainty**: Resample prompts (with replacement) 1 000 times, recompute τ, QET, marginal gains, and correlations; report 95 % confidence intervals. <br>5. **Decision‑rule exploration**: Derive a simple threshold policy: if τ < τ₀ (i.e., the model stabilizes quickly) allocate extra compute to **sampling + reranking**; otherwise allocate to **more denoising steps**. Optimise τ₀ on a held‑out 20 % of the prompt set via grid search and report the resulting QET improvement over a uniform‑step baseline. | Analysis (CPU): ≈ 2 days. <br>Bootstrap (1 000 × lightweight recompute): ≈ 6 GPU‑hrs (can run overnight on a single GPU). |

### Why This Is Substantively Different  
- **Focus**: Instead of measuring *static similarity* between DLM and AR representations (CKA, SVCCA, linear probes, or logit KL), we quantify a **dynamical property of the DLM’s own inference process**—how fast its denoising trajectory stabilizes.  
- **Mechanistic Link**: The stabilization speed τ is hypothesized to be a direct consequence of how much AR knowledge the DLM has retained; a well‑aligned model settles quickly because its updates are already pulling toward AR‑consistent predictions.  
- **Analytical Novelty**: We relate τ to the *marginal QET gains* of two distinct compute‑allocation strategies (more denoising steps vs. more samples + AR reranking) using mixed‑effects modeling and bootstrap inference, providing a causal‑flavored, decision‑rule‑oriented outcome rather than a mere correlation report.  
- **Practical Utility**: The resulting threshold τ₀ yields a simple, runtime‑usable heuristic: “if the model’s denoising trajectory stabilizes within X steps, spend extra compute on sampling + reranking; otherwise, spend it on additional denoising steps.” This can be applied without any extra training or probing pipelines, fitting strictly within the inference‑only constraint.  

This method therefore offers a fresh, rigorous, and generalizable pathway to answer the original research question while respecting all feasibility constraints.
