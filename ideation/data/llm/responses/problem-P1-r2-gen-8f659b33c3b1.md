Problem:  
Does the extent to which a diffusion language model (DLM) preserves the autoregressive (AR) representations of its pretrained backbone determine how much a lightweight post‑hoc selection mechanism (e.g., reranking with a small AR scorer) can improve the quality‑efficiency trade‑off, and does this relationship vary across model families and denoising‑step budgets?

Rationale:  
The target paper shows that continual pre‑training of open‑source AR models yields competitive DLMs (DiffuGPT, DiffuLLaMA) but leaves a sizable “accuracy headroom” (L6) that could be closed either by more denoising steps or by better use of the model’s inherent candidate diversity. Recent work demonstrates inference‑time compute‑quality trade‑offs via methods such as Jacobi Forcing (self‑distillation of parallel decoding trajectories) and TESS 2’s reward guidance, yet it remains unclear whether a simple, training‑free selection step—applying a tiny AR model to score and rerank multiple diffusion samples—can recover this headroom more efficiently than additional denoising, and why some DLMs might benefit more than others.  

To answer this, we will (1) quantify how closely each released DLM’s internal representations match those of its AR base model (using centered kernel alignment (CKA) or linear probing on a held‑out text corpus), thereby obtaining a measure of AR‑representation preservation; (2) generate multiple candidates per prompt at a range of denoising‑step budgets (e.g., 16, 32, 64, 128 steps) using the released checkpoints (DiffuGPT‑S/M, DiffuLLaMA, Dream‑7B, LLaDA‑8B, DiffuCoder‑7B); (3) rerank these candidates with a lightweight AR scorer (e.g., GPT‑2‑small, 124 M parameters, contributing <1 % of total FLOPs and negligible latency); (4) evaluate quality with a unified metric— the average of normalized scores across HumanEval pass@k (k=1,10), GSM8K accuracy, and SIQA/WinoGrande commonsense scores— and efficiency with wall‑clock latency per token and estimated FLOPs (generation × model size + scorer overhead); and (5) analyze whether higher representation‑preservation scores correlate with larger quality gains from selection at fixed compute budgets, and whether this correlation differs between GPT‑2‑based and LLaMA‑based DLMs or changes with step budget.  

This study stays strictly within the inference‑only constraint (no training, adaptation, or continual pre‑training), uses only released checkpoints and publicly available base models for probing, and fits the ten‑week timeline on a single RTX 6000 Pro Blackwell by limiting generation to ≤128 steps (where marginal returns are most informative) and parallelizing candidate generation, scoring, and evaluation across team members. By linking representation‑preservation analysis to the effectiveness of lightweight selection, the work moves beyond an empirical optimization study to provide fundamental insight into how AR knowledge encoded in a DLM’s hidden structure governs the utility of its candidate set—thereby clarifying whether the accuracy headroom stems from insufficient denoising or from suboptimal exploitation of AR‑derived representations. This bridges the gap between representation‑alignment techniques (e.g., REPR‑ALIGN, PreDiff‑LM) and pure inference‑time trade‑off methods (Jacobi Forcing, TESS 2), offering a principled guide for allocating inference compute between denoising and selection when deploying scaled diffusion language models.
