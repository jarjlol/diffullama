Review: The research problem is well-scoped for the given resource constraints and is fundamentally sound in its experimental design. It is explicitly inference-only, which perfectly aligns with constraint D-2026-09-21-a, and all required checkpoints are publicly available. The methodology—varying candidate count N, fixing denoising budgets, using off-the-shelf AR verifiers, and measuring both accuracy and compute-normalized efficiency—is clearly defined and systematically tractable. The team composition (3 GPU-enabled, 4 CPU-only) maps well onto the task requirements, and the 10-week timeline is reasonable for an inference-only ablation study of this scope. The problem builds directly on the "Diffuse Thinking" framework (related paper #7) and the target paper's own observation of the hit-rate–accuracy mismatch, providing strong existing research support.

Feedback: The primary concern is the **shared and contended single RTX 6000 Pro Blackwell**. Running multiple diffusion checkpoints (DiffuGPT-S/M, DiffuLLaMA 6.74B, Dream-7B, DiffuCoder-7B, LLaDA-8B) across N ∈ {1, 4, 16, 64} candidates, multiple verifier sizes, and several benchmarks (GSM8K, BBH subset) multiplies the experimental volume considerably. With 64 candidates at 64 denoising steps on a 6.74B model, individual experiments could be slow, and GPU contention could extend wall-clock time beyond expectations. It is recommended to (i) prioritize a subset of checkpoints and verifiers for the full N-sweep, (ii) use 4-bit quantization aggressively and validate that quality degradation is negligible for the verifier task, and (iii) front-load the most computationally expensive configurations early in the timeline to allow for contingency. Additionally, the choice of AR verifier strategy (next-token likelihood vs. classification head) should be justified or piloted quickly, as this design decision affects both the interpretation and the computational overhead of the verification stage. These are logistical and planning challenges, not fundamental barriers.
