Problem:  
How does the number of diffusion‑generated reasoning candidates and the choice of an autoregressive verifier affect the quality‑efficiency trade‑off of adapted diffusion language models, and does this relationship vary across model families (GPT‑2‑based vs. LLaMA‑based) and scales (127 M – 7 B)?

Rationale:  
The target paper shows that adapted diffusion language models (DLMs) are competitive with their autoregressive (AR) counterparts but remain undertrained, leaving a noticeable gap in reasoning accuracy (L7). Moreover, the paper’s own analysis reveals that the hit‑rate over generated candidates substantially exceeds single‑answer accuracy on reasoning benchmarks (L6), indicating that the model often generates the correct answer among multiple samples but fails to select it reliably. Recent work (Dream 7B, UNIFUSION, dLLM) has demonstrated that test‑time computation—such as increasing denoising steps or employing adaptive noise schedules—can improve quality, yet the complementary strategy of generating multiple candidates and reranking them with an external verifier has not been systematically explored for adapted DLMs. The “Diffuse Thinking” framework (related paper #7) already proposes using diffusion models as efficient thought proposers and autoregressive models as evaluators, but it has not been applied to the specific adapted DLMs released by the target paper (DiffuGPT, DiffuLLaMA, etc.) nor examined across model families and scales.

To address this gap, we will treat the adapted DLMs as proposal generators and evaluate several off‑the‑shelf AR verifiers (e.g., GPT‑2‑small/medium, LLaMA‑7B) that are publicly available. For each diffusion checkpoint we will sample N independent reasoning chains (varying N ∈ {1, 4, 16, 64}) using a fixed denoising‑step budget (e.g., 64 steps) and then let the verifier score each chain (via next‑token likelihood or a simple classification head) to select the final answer. Quality will be measured by reasoning accuracy on GSM8K and a subset of Big‑Bench‑Hard (BBH) tasks, as well as pass@k metrics to quantify the raw proposal capability. Efficiency will be captured by two complementary metrics: (i) the total number of forward passes required (denoising passes × N + verification passes per candidate) and (ii) wall‑clock time per solved problem on the RTX 6000 Pro Blackwell (with 4‑bit quantization where needed). By plotting quality versus these efficiency metrics we can identify Pareto‑optimal configurations and test whether a modest increase in test‑time computation (more candidates + a lightweight verifier) can close the gap to the AR baselines without retraining.

This study is inference‑only: all required diffusion checkpoints (DiffuGPT‑S/M, DiffuLLaMA 6.74B, Dream‑7B, DiffuCoder‑7B, LLaDA‑8B) are publicly available, and the AR verifiers can be downloaded from Hugging Face. The compute demand fits within a single RTX 6000 Pro Blackwell—batch sampling of multiple candidates is feasible with 4‑bit quantization, and verification can be streamed with minimal overhead. The timeline (ten weeks) allows implementation of a unified sampling/evaluation harness, systematic sweeps over N and verifier size, thorough analysis, and write‑up. The team’s three GPU‑enabled members can handle sampling and verification, while the four CPU‑only members prepare prompts, compute metrics, and conduct statistical analysis.

By explicitly linking the underexplored test‑time selection strategy to the observed hit‑rate‑accuracy mismatch (L6) and the undertraining issue (L7), and by providing compute‑normalized efficiency measures (addressing L9), the work offers a clear, feasible, and significant extension of the target paper’s adaptation recipe. It also bridges the gap between pure diffusion generation and hybrid AR‑DLM reasoning architectures, suggesting a practical recipe for deploying scaled diffusion language models in resource‑sensitive settings where test‑time computation can be traded for improved accuracy.
