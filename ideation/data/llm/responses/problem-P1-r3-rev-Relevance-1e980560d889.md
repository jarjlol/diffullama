Review: The research problem is well-positioned within the current diffusion language modeling landscape. It sits at a productive intersection of two active research threads—representation alignment during AR-to-DLM adaptation (as explored in REPR-ALIGN, PreDiff-LM, and the target paper) and inference-time compute allocation strategies (as explored in Jacobi Forcing and TESS 2). The core hypothesis—that the degree of AR representation preservation in a DLM should predict which inference strategy (more denoising steps vs. sampling + lightweight AR reranking) yields better quality-efficiency trade-offs—is novel, well-motivated, and addresses a genuine gap: no existing work systematically links representation preservation metrics to inference-time decision-making. The problem is clearly defined, the methodology is concrete and feasible, and the expected impact—a principled guideline for practitioners—has practical value.

However, there are notable limitations that prevent this from being a truly groundbreaking problem. First, the conceptual framing is somewhat derivative: it combines existing ideas (CKA-based representation measurement + inference-time strategy comparison) rather than introducing a fundamentally new paradigm. The core intuition that "more compute → better quality" is not novel; the novelty lies in the specific interaction being tested. Second, the empirical design uses only three models for correlation analysis, which severely limits statistical power and generalizability—correlations across three data points are fragile and may not support the strong claims implied by the hypothesis. Third, the problem does not fully engage with nuances from the related literature, such as UNIFUSION's generalized KL objective connections or Dream 7B's noise rescheduling techniques, which could complicate the relationship between representation preservation and inference behavior. Finally, the assumption that representation preservation (as measured by CKA) is the primary determinant of the quality-efficiency trade-off may oversimplify a more multifaceted relationship involving model architecture, training dynamics, and task characteristics.

Feedback: The problem is relevant and well-connected, demonstrating a good understanding of the existing literature and offering a promising, testable contribution. To strengthen it further, I would recommend: (1) expanding the model pool beyond three checkpoints to include more diverse architectures (e.g., BERT-based DLMs, smaller-scale models) to improve statistical robustness; (2) explicitly addressing potential confounds such as the interaction between model scale, architecture family, and task type on the representation-preservation ↔ inference-strategy relationship; (3) engaging more deeply with UNIFUSION's unified objective framework and PreDiff-LM's hybrid attention findings, which may complicate the simple preservation-quality narrative; (4) considering whether the tiny AR scorer (GPT-2-small) is appropriately matched to the DLM being reranked, or whether scorer quality itself interacts with representation preservation; and (5) framing the contribution more precisely as an empirical bridge rather than implying a general principle, given the limited scope of the experimental design.
