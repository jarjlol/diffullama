**Review:**

The research problem is clearly formulated and well-motivated within the current landscape of diffusion language modeling. It addresses a genuinely relevant practical question: how should inference compute be allocated between denoising steps and lightweight candidate selection when deploying scaled DLMs? The rationale effectively anchors the problem in findings from the target paper (DiffuLLaMA) and related work (Jacobi Forcing, TESS 2, Dream 7B), and the experimental design—generating candidates at multiple step budgets and reranking with frozen small AR scorers—is feasible and well-scoped.

However, the significance of this problem is tempered by several considerations. First, the core idea—using a cheap classifier or scorer to filter outputs from a more expensive generative model—is a well-established pattern in machine learning broadly (e.g., cascading classifiers, reranking in retrieval). While its specific application to DLMs is timely, the conceptual novelty is modest. Second, several recent papers (notably Jacobi Forcing and TESS 2) already implicitly or explicitly address quality-efficiency trade-offs in diffusion LMs, meaning the proposed study risks overlapping with or being incremental to existing efforts rather than opening a genuinely new direction. Third, the problem is fundamentally an empirical optimization study rather than one that advances theoretical understanding of DLMs; the claim that it will "shed light on whether accuracy headroom is a symptom of insufficient denoising or suboptimal candidate usage" is plausible but may yield practical guidance rather than fundamental insight.

The cross-model-family comparison (GPT-2 vs. LLaMA) and step-budget dependence analysis are the strongest elements, as they could reveal non-trivial architectural and scaling insights. Nevertheless, the overall impact is likely to be confined to the practical deployment niche of DLM inference, without broader transformative implications for the field.

**Feedback:**
- **Strengths:** The problem is clearly defined, empirically feasible, and addresses a real practical need in DLM deployment. The multi-dimensional analysis (selection vs. steps, model-family differences, step-budget dependence) provides a solid experimental framework.
- **Weaknesses:** The core idea lacks strong novelty—it is an application of a well-known ML pattern (cheap scorer filtering expensive generator outputs) to a new domain. The problem risks being perceived as incremental relative to recent work that already touches on quality-efficiency trade-offs. The significance claim overstates potential theoretical contribution; the study is more likely to yield practical guidelines than fundamental advances.
- **Suggestions for improvement:** To elevate significance, the problem could be reframed to target a deeper question—e.g., *what structural properties of the candidate set, as determined by the adapted AR backbone, govern the effectiveness of lightweight selection?* This would shift the work from an engineering study to one with clearer theoretical implications about how autoregressive knowledge is preserved and exploitable in diffusion models. Additionally, explicitly contrasting the proposed approach against Jacobi Forcing and reward guidance (TESS 2) at a conceptual level would strengthen the positioning.

**Rating (1-5): 3**

The problem demonstrates average significance: it has clear practical implications and contributes useful empirical evidence to the DLM literature, but it lacks the novelty or broader impact needed for a higher rating. It is a solid, well-motivated applied study rather than a groundbreaking contribution.
