**Method**

| Step | Goal | Concrete actions | Resources & Constraints |
|------|------|------------------|--------------------------|
| **0. Preparation** | Set up a reproducible, inference‑only pipeline that respects the hardware and timeline limits. | • Install the released checkpoints (DiffuGPT‑S/M, DiffuLLaMA‑6.74B, Dream‑7B, LLaDA‑8B, DiffuCoder‑7B) and the corresponding AR base models (GPT‑2‑small/medium, LLaMA‑7B). <br>• Prepare a *held‑out probing corpus* (e.g., 10 k sentences randomly sampled from WikiText‑103 + C4, tokenised with each model’s tokenizer). <br>• Prepare the downstream benchmark suite (HumanEval, GSM8K, SIQA, WinoGrande) and a script that normalises each metric to \([0,1]\) across all conditions. <br>• Implement activation‑checkpointing and 8‑bit quantisation (via `bitsandbytes`) for the 6.74B DiffuLLaMA so that it fits in the 96 GB GPU with room for batch‑size ≥ 4. <br>• Parallelise across the three GPU‑enabled team members: each member owns a subset of models/step‑budgets and writes results to a shared CSV/Parquet store. | One RTX 6000 Pro Blackwell (96 GB), 8‑bit quantisation, activation checkpointing, CPU‑only work for data prep/eval harnesses. |
| **1. Quantify AR‑representation preservation** | Obtain a scalar “preservation score” for each DLM that reflects how closely its hidden‑state geometry matches that of its AR backbone. | **1.1 Feature extraction** – For every sentence in the probing corpus, run the DLM **inference‑only** (no gradient) and collect the hidden‑state tensor of each Transformer layer (post‑attention, before the feed‑forward). Do the same with the frozen AR base model (same tokenizer, same sequence length). <br>**1.2 Alignment metrics** – Compute two complementary similarity scores per layer and then aggregate: <br> • **Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA)** (linear kernel) between the DLM and AR hidden‑state matrices (shape: *N × D*). <br> • **Linear probing**: train a ridge‑regression probe (no gradient to the DLM) to predict the AR hidden states from the DLM hidden states; record the coefficient of determination \(R^2\). <br>**1.3 Aggregation** – Average CKA (or \(R^2\)) across layers, weighting deeper layers more (e.g., exponential decay) because they carry higher‑level semantics. The final scalar is the *representation‑preservation score* \(P_i\) for model \(i\). | No training; only forward passes. Linear probe is solved analytically (ridge regression) on CPU. |
| **2. Multi‑candidate generation** | Produce a set of diverse outputs for each prompt at several denoising‑step budgets. | • Choose a prompt set that covers the three benchmark families (e.g., 200 HumanEval prompts, 200 GSM8K questions, 200 SIQA/WinoGrande items). <br>• For each DLM and each denoising‑step budget \(S \in \{16,32,64,128\}\): <br> - Sample **K = 8** candidates per prompt using *top‑p (p=0.9)* nucleus sampling (ensures diversity while keeping the distribution close to the model’s). <br> - Store the raw token sequences and the per‑step denoising trace (optional for later analysis). <br>• Use the same random seed across models to make candidate sets comparable. | Generation is pure inference; batch size limited by GPU memory (≈ 4‑8 sequences per step). |
| **3. Lightweight, training‑free reranking** | Apply a tiny AR scorer to reorder the candidates and compute the gain from selection. | • Load **GPT‑2‑small (124 M)** as the scorer (frozen). <br>• For each candidate, compute the *average negative log‑likelihood* (or perplexity) under the scorer; lower score = higher likelihood. <br>• Rank candidates ascending by this score; the top‑1 after reranking is the *selected* output. <br>• Record the scorer’s FLOPs (≈ 0.3 % of a single denoising step for the largest DLM) and wall‑clock time (measured on the same GPU). | Scorer fits easily in memory; its contribution to latency is negligible (< 1 ms per candidate). |
| **4. Quality measurement** | Translate raw outputs into a unified, comparable metric. | • For each benchmark: <br> - **HumanEval**: compute pass@k for k = 1 and k = 10 (using the standard unit‑test harness). <br> - **GSM8K**: exact match accuracy after answer extraction. <br> - **SIQA & WinoGrande**: accuracy (multiple‑choice). <br>• Normalise each raw score to \([0,1]\) across *all* model‑step‑budget‑reranking conditions (min‑max scaling). <br>• Define the **Unified Quality Score (UQS)** as the arithmetic mean of the four normalised components (HumanEval@1, HumanEval@10, GSM8K, (SIQA+WinoGrande)/2). <br>• Report also the *oracle* UQS (the best candidate among the K generated) to quantify the *accuracy headroom* for each condition. | All metrics are computed offline on CPU; no extra GPU load. |
| **5. Experimental contrasts** | Isolate the two sources of improvement: (a) reranking vs. (b) more denoising steps. | For each DLM \(i\) and each base step budget \(S_{low}=16\): <br> - **Reranking gain**: \(\Delta^{\text{rank}}_{i,S_{low}} = \text{UQS}_{i,S_{low}}^{\text{ranked}} - \text{UQS}_{i,S_{low}}^{\text{top‑1}}\). <br> - **Denoising‑step gain** (no reranking): \(\Delta^{\text{step}}_{i} = \text{UQS}_{i,128}^{\text{top‑1}} - \text{UQS}_{i,16}^{\text{top‑1}}\). <br>• Compute the *relative efficiency* as \(\eta_i = \frac{\Delta^{\text{rank}}_{i,S_{low}}}{\Delta^{\text{step}}_{i}}\) (values > 1 indicate reranking yields more quality per unit of added compute). | Requires only the four step budgets; no additional model runs. |
| **6. Statistical modelling** | Test whether preservation predicts reranking advantage and whether this differs by architecture or step budget. | Fit a **linear mixed‑effects model** (LME) with UQS as the dependent variable: <br>\[
\text{UQS}_{ijk\ell}= \beta_0 + \beta_1 P_i + \beta_2 S_j + \beta_3 R_{k} + \beta_4 (P_i \times S_j) + \beta_5 (P_i \times R_{k}) + \beta_6 (S_j \times R_{k}) + u_i + \epsilon_{ijk\ell}
\] <br>where: <br>• \(P_i\) = preservation score (continuous). <br>• \(S_j\) = denoising‑step budget (treated as log‑scaled continuous). <br>• \(R_k\) = reranking indicator (0 = top‑1, 1 = reranked). <br>• \(u_i\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2_{model})\) captures model‑specific random intercepts (accounts for unobserved heterogeneity). <br>• \(\epsilon\) is residual error. <br>Key hypotheses: <br> - **H1**: \(\beta_1 > 0\) (higher preservation → higher baseline quality). <br> - **H2**: \(\beta_4 > 0\) (the benefit of extra steps grows with preservation). <br> - **H3**: \(\beta_5 > 0\) (reranking yields larger gains when preservation is high). <br> - **H4**: Interaction with architecture (add a dummy variable for GPT‑2‑based vs LLaMA‑based DLMs and test \(\beta_{5,\text{arch}}\)). <br>Use restricted maximum likelihood (REML) for parameter estimation; report likelihood‑ratio tests and 95 % confidence intervals. <br>Additionally, compute Spearman’s \(\rho\) between \(P_i\) and \(\eta_i\) (relative efficiency) to directly address the research question. | All modelling performed on CPU (e.g., `statsmodels` or `lme4` via R). |
| **7. Efficiency accounting** | Translate quality gains into comparable compute cost. | • Measure wall‑clock time for a single denoising step (averaged over 100 runs) for each DLM (with activation checkpointing & 8‑bit quant). <br>• Compute **cost per step**: \(C^{\text{step}}_i = t^{\text{step}}_i\). <br>• Compute **cost of reranking**: \(C^{\text{rank}}_i = K \times t^{\text{scorer}}_i\) (scorer time per candidate). <br>• Define **efficiency ratio**: \(\epsilon_i = \frac{\Delta^{\text{rank}}_{i,S_{low}} / C^{\text{rank}}_i}{\Delta^{\text{step}}_{i} / (C^{\text{step}}_i \times (128-16))}\). <br>Values > 1 indicate reranking gives more quality per FLOP/second than adding denoising steps. | Timing measurements are cheap (< 5 min total). |
| **8. Ablation & robustness checks** | Verify that findings are not artefacts of specific choices. | • Vary the scorer size (GPT‑2‑medium, DistilGPT‑2) to confirm < 1 % FLOP claim. <br>• Replace CKA with **Procrustes similarity** or **SVCCA** as alternative preservation metrics. <br>• Change nucleus sampling p (0.8, 0.95) and K (4, 16) to test sensitivity to candidate diversity. <br>• Run a *control* where the AR scorer is replaced by a random scorer to ensure gains are not due to chance ordering. | All ablations reuse the same generated candidate pool; no extra generation needed. |
| **9. Reporting & reproducibility** | Ensure transparent dissemination. | • Archive all scripts, random seeds, and environment specifications (Dockerfile or Conda yaml). <br>• Publish the extracted hidden‑state features (optional) and the final results table on an open repository (e.g., HuggingFace datasets + Zenodo). <br>• Provide a short “re‑run” notebook that reproduces the key figures (preservation vs. reranking gain, efficiency ratio). | Fits the ten‑week timeline: weeks 1‑2 for setup, weeks 3‑5 for generation & scoring, week 6 for metric computation, weeks 7‑8 for statistical analysis, weeks 9‑10 for write‑up and reproducibility packaging. |

**Rationale**

The proposed method directly targets the core research question: *Does the extent to which a diffusion language model (DLM) retains the autoregressive (AR) representations of its pretrained backbone determine how much a lightweight, training‑free reranking step can recover the model’s accuracy headroom, and is this recovery more efficient than simply adding denoising steps?*  

1. **Linking representation preservation to reranking potential** – By quantifying the similarity between a DLM’s hidden states and those of its AR backbone (CKA + linear probing), we obtain a continuous, model‑specific proxy for how much of the AR linguistic knowledge survived the diffusion conversion. Prior work (REPR‑ALIGN, PreDiff‑LM) shows that preserving AR representations improves training efficiency; here we test whether the same preservation also makes the model’s *output distribution* more amenable to post‑hoc selection by an AR scorer.  

2. **Isolating the two improvement levers** – The experimental design creates a factorial grid: (i) model architecture (GPT‑2‑based vs LLaMA‑based), (ii) denoising‑step budget (low vs high), and (iii) presence/absence of reranking. This lets us decompose the total quality gain into (a) *selection gain* (reranking over a fixed candidate set) and (b) *denoising gain* (more steps, no reranking). The mixed‑effects model explicitly tests whether preservation moderates the selection gain (interaction \(P_i \times R_k\)) while controlling for step‑budget effects and model‑level random variation.  

3. **Efficiency‑centric comparison** – Rather than reporting raw accuracy differences, we normalise quality gains by the actual compute cost of each lever (wall‑clock time/FLOPs). The efficiency ratio \(\epsilon_i\) answers the practical question: *If we have a fixed inference budget, should we spend it on extra denoising steps or on a tiny AR reranker?* This directly addresses the “more efficient than simply increasing the denoising‑step budget” clause of the problem.  

4. **Adherence to inference‑only constraint** – All steps rely solely on forward passes of released checkpoints. The only learned component is the ridge‑regression probe, which is solved analytically on CPU and does not modify any model weights. No continual pre‑training, fine‑tuning, or adaptation is performed, satisfying the team decision D‑2026‑09‑21‑a.  

5. **Scalability within the given resources** – Activation checkpointing and 8‑bit quantisation enable the largest model (DiffuLLaMA‑6.74B) to run on a single 96 GB GPU. Generation is limited to ≤128 steps, a range where the target paper reports informative marginal returns. Parallelising across three GPU‑enabled team members keeps wall‑clock time well under the ten‑week limit, while CPU‑heavy tasks (tokenisation, probing, evaluation, statistical modelling) are distributed to the remaining four members.  

6. **Generalisability** – The protocol is model‑agnostic: any DLM released with an associated AR base can be plugged in. The preservation metrics (CKA, linear probing, alternatives) and the unified quality metric (HumanEval, GSM8K, SIQA, WinoGrande) are standard benchmarks, making the findings comparable to future DLMs. The mixed‑effects framework readily accommodates additional architectures (e.g., Mamba‑based diffusion) or alternative lightweight scorers (e.g., TinyLlama) without redesign.  

7. **Novelty beyond empirical tuning** – While prior inference‑time methods (Jacobi Forcing, TESS 2 reward guidance) modify the generation process, our approach treats the *generation* and *selection* stages as separable, quantifies the intrinsic representation fidelity of the generator, and asks whether that fidelity predicts the *utility* of a cheap post‑hoc selector. This bridges representation‑alignment research (which focuses on training) with pure inference‑time trade‑off research, offering a mechanistic explanation for why some DLMs benefit more from reranking than others.  

In summary, the method provides a **rigorous, reproducible, and computationally feasible** pipeline that measures representation preservation, isolates the effects of reranking versus extra denoising, evaluates efficiency, and tests the hypothesised relationship with statistical models—all while staying strictly within the inference‑only, single‑GPU, ten‑week constraints. The resulting insights will inform whether future efforts to close the accuracy headroom of DLMs should prioritize better preservation of AR representations (e.g., via alignment objectives) or smarter, lightweight selection mechanisms.
