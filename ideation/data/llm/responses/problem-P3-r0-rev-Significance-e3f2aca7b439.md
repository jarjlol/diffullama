**Review:**

The research problem addresses a genuine but relatively narrow gap in the diffusion language modeling literature: the systematic study of how noise schedule shape affects the quality-efficiency trade-off of AR-adapted DLMs across model families and scales. The problem is well-motivated by the target paper's limitations and supported by recent work (Dream 7B, UNIFUSION) that hints at the importance of schedule design. The feasibility analysis is thorough, and the inference-only nature of the study makes it practically achievable.

However, the significance of this problem is constrained by several factors. First, the investigation is fundamentally an empirical ablation of inference-time hyperparameters (noise schedules) rather than a contribution of new methods, theories, or paradigms. While identifying optimal schedules is practically useful, it does not advance the foundational understanding of diffusion language modeling. Second, the claim that findings could help "close the gap between adapted DLMs and their AR counterparts" appears overstated—tuning the sampling schedule alone is unlikely to resolve structural quality gaps arising from attention-mask design, training objectives, or architectural mismatches. Third, the problem is somewhat derivative: Dream 7B and UNIFUSION have already demonstrated the impact of context-adaptive scheduling, so the proposed study largely extends existing observations to additional scales and architectures without a clear novelty claim beyond systematic empirical comparison.

**Feedback:**

The problem is well-defined and feasible, and it fills a concrete gap in the empirical literature on DLMs. To strengthen its significance, consider: (1) articulating a clearer scientific question beyond "which schedule is best"—e.g., *why* certain schedules interact differently with model scale or architecture, potentially linking to representational properties of the adapted checkpoints; (2) connecting the noise schedule analysis to broader questions about the diffusion process itself (e.g., how schedule shape relates to the loss landscape or the quality of the reverse-chain); and (3) tempering claims about "closing the gap" with AR models, as this overpromises what an inference-time ablation can achieve. As stated, the problem is a solid empirical contribution but lacks the transformative or theoretically grounding ambition that would elevate its significance beyond moderate levels.
