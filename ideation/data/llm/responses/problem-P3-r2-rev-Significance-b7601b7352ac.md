Review: The research problem is well-formulated and addresses a genuine, underexplored aspect of diffusion language modeling—how the temporal pattern of attention masking during sampling interacts with model scale and architecture. The problem is clearly motivated by specific limitations identified in the target paper and related work, and the operationalization of quality-efficiency trade-offs is concrete and measurable. The inference-only nature of the study and the feasibility argument are credible given publicly available checkpoints. The hypothesis about scale-dependent interactions between causal and bidirectional attention during denoising is mechanistically plausible and adds intellectual depth beyond a routine ablation.

However, the problem's significance is tempered by several considerations. At its core, the study is an empirical investigation of a specific inference-time hyperparameter (the attention-mask schedule) and its effect on performance. While the systematic nature and mechanistic framing elevate it above a simple sweep, the potential for groundbreaking or transformative impact is limited. The problem does not address fundamental challenges in DLM training, architecture design, or scaling laws; rather, it refines an existing adaptation pipeline. The overlap with related work on noise scheduling and attention mechanisms (e.g., PreDiff-LM's hybrid attention, UNIFUSION's unified objectives) means the contribution may be viewed as a complementary refinement rather than a novel paradigm. Additionally, the claim that the study offers "mechanistic understanding" somewhat overstates what a mask-schedule ablation can deliver—it is primarily empirical evidence about which configurations work better, not a deep theoretical account of why diffusion processes interact with causal structure.

Feedback: The problem is clearly articulated, well-motivated, and feasible, with practical implications for DLM deployment. To strengthen its significance, the authors could consider: (1) explicitly framing how findings might inform future *adaptation recipes* or *architectural designs* beyond inference-time scheduling, thereby broadening impact; (2) connecting the mask-schedule analysis to broader questions about the role of causal inductive bias in diffusion-based generation, which would elevate the work from engineering optimization to conceptual contribution; and (3) clarifying how results might transfer across model families and scales in a way that generalizes beyond the specific checkpoints under study.

Rating (1-5): 3
