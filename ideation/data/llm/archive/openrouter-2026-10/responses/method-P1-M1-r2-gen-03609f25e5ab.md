Quantify AR‑representation preservation** | Obtain a scalar “preservation score” \(P_i\) for each DLM that reflects similarity of its hidden‑state geometry to that of its AR backbone. | **1.1 Feature extraction** – For every sentence in the probing corpus, run the DLM **inference‑only** (no gradient) and collect the hidden‑state tensor of each Transformer layer **after the attention sub‑layer and before the feed‑forward** (the residual stream). Do the same with the frozen AR base model (same token IDs, same sequence length). <br>**1.2 Similarity metrics per layer** – Compute both: <br> • **Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA)** with a linear kernel between the DLM and AR hidden‑state matrices (\(N\times D\)). <br> • **Linear probing**: fit a ridge‑regression model (no gradient to the DLM) to predict AR hidden states from DLM hidden states; record the coefficient of determination \(R^2\). <br>**1.3 Layer weighting** – Assign weight \(w_\ell = \exp\bigl(-\lambda \cdot \frac{L-\ell}{L}\bigr)\) to layer \(\ell\) (where \(L\) = total number of layers, \(\ell=1\) is the first layer). Do the same for \(R^2\) to obtain \(P_i^{\text{probe}}\). The primary preservation score is \(P_i = P_i^{\text{CKA}}\); the probing score is retained as a secondary, complementary measure. | No training; only forward passes. Ridge regression solved analytically on CPU (scikit‑learn). |
| **2. Multi‑candidate generation** | Produce a diverse set of outputs for each prompt at several denoising‑step budgets. | • Build a prompt set that mirrors the three benchmark families: 200 HumanEval prompts, 200 GSM8K questions, 200 SIQA/WinoGrande items (total 600 prompts). <br>• For each DLM and each denoising‑step budget \(S \in \{16,32,64,128\}\): <br> - Sample **K = 8** candidates per prompt using nucleus sampling with \(p=0.9\) (ensures diversity while staying close to the model distribution). <br> - Store the raw token sequences; optionally keep the per‑step denoising trace for later analysis. <br>• Use the same global random seed across all models so that candidate sets are comparable. | Generation is pure inference; batch size limited by GPU memory (≈ 4‑8 sequences per step). |
| **3. Lightweight, training‑free reranking** | Apply a tiny AR scorer to reorder candidates and compute the selection gain. | • **Primary scorer:** GPT‑2‑small (124 M parameters). <br>• **Cross‑family scorer (ablation):** a distilled LLaMA‑7B model (≈ 1 B parameters after distillation) or LLaMA‑7B if memory permits; this scorer is used only to test whether the preservation‑gain relationship holds when the scorer architecture differs from the DLM’s backbone. <br>• For each candidate, compute the **average negative log‑likelihood** (or perplexity) under the scorer; lower score = higher likelihood. <br>• Rank candidates ascending by this score; the top‑1 after reranking is the *selected* output. <br>• Measure the scorer’s FLOPs and wall‑clock time (≈ 0.3 % of a single denoising step for the largest DLM; < 1 ms per candidate). | Scorers fit easily in memory; latency contribution negligible (< 1 ms per candidate). |

| **5. Experimental contrasts** | Isolate the two sources of improvement: (a) reranking vs. (b) more denoising steps. | For each DLM \(i\) and low step budget \(S_{\text{low}}=16\): <br> - **Reranking gain**: \(\Delta^{\text{rank}}_{i,S_{\text{low}}} = \text{UQS}_{i,S_{\text{low}}}^{\text{ranked}} - \text{UQS}_{i,S_{\text{low}}}^{\text{top‑1}}\). <br> - **Denoising‑step gain** (no reranking): \(\Delta^{\text{step}}_{i} = \text{UQS}_{i,128}^{\text{top‑1}} - \text{UQS}_{i,16}^{\text{top‑1}}\). <br>• **Relative efficiency (gain‑ratio)**: \(\eta_i = \dfrac{\Delta^{\text{rank}}_{i,S_{\text{low}}}}{\Delta^{\text{step}}_{i}}\) (values > 1 indicate reranking yields more quality per added denoising step). <br>• **Compute‑normalised efficiency**: measure wall‑clock time per denoising step \(t^{\text{step}}_i\) (averaged over 100 runs) and scorer time per candidate \(t^{\text{scorer}}_i\). Define <br> \(\epsilon_i = \dfrac{\Delta^{\text{rank}}_{i,S_{\text{low}}} / (K \cdot t^{\text{scorer}}_i)}{\Delta^{\text{step}}_{i} / \bigl(t^{\text{step}}_i \times (128-16)\bigr)}\). <br> Values > 1 mean reranking delivers more quality per unit of compute than adding denoising steps. | Requires only the four step budgets; no additional model runs. **Rationale:**  
The revised protocol directly targets the core question: *Does the extent to which a diffusion language model (DLM) retains the autoregressive (AR) representations of its pretrained backbone determine how much a lightweight, training‑free reranking step can recover the model’s accuracy headroom, and is this recovery more efficient than simply adding denoising steps?*  

1. **Linking representation preservation to reranking potential** – We compute CKA (a symmetric, scale‑invariant similarity measure) and linear probing on a held‑out text corpus. CKA is taken as the primary preservation score because it captures geometric similarity without requiring a predictive model, thereby reducing bias toward the scorer architecture. The linear‑probe score is retained as a complementary, interpretable check. Layer‑wise aggregation uses an explicit exponential decay (\(\lambda\)) that gives deeper semantic layers higher influence, a choice justified by prior probing work (e.g., Mahabadi et al., 2021) and varied in ablation.  

2. **Isolating the two improvement levers** – By generating multiple candidates at four denoising‑step budgets and applying a tiny AR scorer, we obtain (a) the *selection gain* (reranking over a fixed candidate set) and (b) the *denoising‑step gain* (more steps, no reranking). The gain‑ratio \(\eta_i\) and the compute‑normalised ratio \(\epsilon_i\) let us compare quality improvement per unit of added compute, directly addressing the efficiency clause of the research question.  

3. **Addressing scorer‑model family confounding** – The primary scorer is GPT‑2‑small for all DLMs, ensuring a consistent, lightweight reference. A cross‑family ablation using a distilled LLaMA‑based scorer tests whether the preservation‑gain relationship holds when the scorer architecture differs from the DLM’s backbone, mitigating circularity concerns.  

4. **Robust statistical inference with limited models** – With only five DLM checkpoints, we avoid over‑parameterised interaction models. Instead, we rely on Spearman correlations and simple linear regressions between the model‑level preservation score and the reranking gains, reporting bias‑corrected bootstrap confidence intervals. A Bayesian regression with weakly informative priors serves as a sensitivity check. This approach acknowledges the exploratory nature of the study while still providing principled uncertainty quantification.  

5. **Unified yet transparent quality metric** – Rather than deriving weights from a small validation set (which can be unstable), we use the simple average of four benchmark‑specific normalised scores (equal weight). This is interpretable, literature‑aligned, and avoids over‑fitting. We additionally report PCA‑derived weights as an exploratory sensitivity analysis. Normalisation uses a *fixed* reference set (the first 10 % of prompts) to break the circular dependency between the metric and the experimental matrix.  

6. **Efficiency accounting made explicit** – We provide both absolute gains and the two efficiency ratios (\(\eta_i\) and \(\epsilon_i\)), clearly defining their denominators (extra denoising steps vs. scorer compute). This removes ambiguity about what “more efficient than increasing the denoising‑step budget” means and allows practitioners to decide where to allocate a fixed inference budget.  

7. **Comprehensive ablation and robustness** – We vary scorer size, alternative preservation metrics, candidate‑diversity settings, normalization pools, hidden‑state extraction points, and even add a generation‑domain probing set to verify that preservation scores are not artefacts of the probing corpus. A random‑scorer control confirms that gains are not due to chance ordering.  

8. **Adherence to inference‑only constraint** – All steps rely solely on forward passes of released checkpoints; the only learned component (ridge regression) is solved analytically on CPU and does not modify any model weights. No continual pre‑training, fine‑tuning, or adaptation is performed, satisfying the team decision D‑2026‑09‑21‑a.  

9. **Feasibility within the given resources** – Activation checkpointing and 8‑bit quantisation enable the 6.74B DiffuLLaMA to run on a single 96 GB GPU with batch size ≥4. Generation is capped at 128 steps, a range where the target paper shows informative marginal returns. Parallelising across three GPU‑enabled team members keeps wall‑clock time well under the ten‑week limit, while CPU‑heavy tasks (feature extraction, probing, evaluation, statistical modelling) are distributed to the remaining four members.  

10. **Generalisability** – The protocol is model‑agnostic: any DLM released with an associated AR base can be plugged in. The preservation metrics (CKA, linear probing, SVCCA, Procrustes) and the lightweight scorers (GPT‑2‑family, LLaMA‑family) are interchangeable. The unified quality metric can be recomputed with alternative benchmarks or weighted schemes, and the statistical framework accommodates additional architectures (e.g., Mamba‑based diffusion) or alternative lightweight scorers without redesign.  

By tightly integrating representation‑alignment analysis with a rigorous, efficiency‑focused comparison of lightweight selection versus extra denoising, this method moves beyond empirical tuning to provide a mechanistic explanation for why some DLMs benefit more from reranking than others. It bridges the representation‑alignment literature (REPR‑ALIGN, PreDiff‑LM) with pure inference‑time trade‑off work (Jacobi Forcing, TESS 2), offering actionable insight for future DLM design and deployment.
