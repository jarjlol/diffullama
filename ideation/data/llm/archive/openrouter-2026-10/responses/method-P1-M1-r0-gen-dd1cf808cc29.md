**Method**

| Step | Action | Details & Innovations | Resources & Timeline |
|------|--------|-----------------------|----------------------|
| **0. Project Setup** | Create a shared repository (Git‑LFS) with conda‑environment yaml, Dockerfile (CUDA 12.4, PyTorch 2.4) and a lightweight workflow manager (e.g., `ray` or `slurm`‑like job scheduler) to distribute GPU jobs among the three GPU‑enabled members. | Guarantees reproducibility and lets CPU‑only teammates run tokenisation, prompt‑stratification, and metric‑aggregation in parallel. | 1 day (setup) |
| **1. Model & Backbone Selection** | Choose **four** released DLMs to increase statistical power while staying within memory limits: <br>• DiffuGPT‑S (127 M, GPT‑2‑based) <br>• DiffuGPT‑M (355 M, GPT‑2‑based) <br>• DiffuLLaMA (6.74 B, LLaMA‑2‑based) <br>• Dream‑7B (7 B, LLaMA‑2‑based) | Adding a second GPT‑2 model lets us test whether the relationship scales with size within the same family. All checkpoints are publicly available; no fine‑tuning is performed (inference‑only). | 0.5 day |
| **2. Representation‑Preservation Quantification** | For each DLM‑backbone pair compute **three complementary similarity scores** on the WikiText‑103 validation split (≈ 5 k sentences): <br>1. **Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA)** – as in the baseline. <br>2. **SVCCA (Singular Vector Canonical Correlation Analysis)** – captures subspace similarity and is less sensitive to isotropic scaling. <br>3. **Linear‑probe AUC** – train a shallow (single‑layer) logistic regression on frozen DLM hidden states to predict the next‑token distribution of the backbone; report macro‑averaged AUC. <br>Combine the three scores into a **Preservation Index (PI)** via z‑score normalisation and taking the mean (or, alternatively, keep them separate for multi‑factor analysis). | Using multiple, orthogonal metrics reduces bias from any single estimator and yields a more robust latent‑space similarity measure. The linear‑probe also provides a task‑specific sanity check that the preserved geometry is useful for next‑token prediction. | Compute CKA & SVCCA in fp16 with activation checkpointing (≈ 2 GPU‑hrs per model). Linear probe runs on CPU (negligible). Total ≈ 8 GPU‑hrs. |
| **3. Prompt Stratification & Batch Construction** | Build a **prompt pool** of 300 instances (100 each from HumanEval, GSM8K, SIQA/WinoGrande) stratified by difficulty (e.g., HumanEval pass@1 baseline < 0.2, 0.2‑0.5, >0.5). For each difficulty tier, create **balanced mini‑batches of size = 4** (mixed domains) to maximise GPU utilisation while keeping variance low. | Stratification enables later analysis of whether the representation‑preservation effect interacts with task difficulty (an exploratory entity: “bidirectional context”, “parallel generation”). | CPU‑side preparation: 1 day. |
| **4. Diffusion Sampling & Candidate Generation** | For each DLM, run the native sampler at three denoising‑step budgets: **Low = 16**, **Medium = 64**, **High = 256** steps. At each budget generate **N ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8}** samples per prompt (different seeds). <br>• Use **fp16** + **activation checkpointing**. <br>• For DiffuLLaMA (6.74 B) additionally test **4‑bit quantisation** (bitsandbytes) *only* for the sampling phase; keep a full‑precision copy for the PI calculation (so quantisation does not contaminate representation measurement). <br>• Store all generated token sequences (as integer IDs) to avoid recomputation for scoring. | The extra N = 2 condition gives a finer granularity for estimating marginal gains. Quantisation ablation lets us verify that the observed trade‑offs are not an artefact of precision loss. | Rough estimate: 300 prompts × 4 (N) × 3 (step budgets) × 4 (models) ≈ 14 400 generations. With batch‑size = 4 and activation checkpointing, wall‑time ≈ 22 GPU‑hrs (well under the 30 GPU‑hr budget). |
| **5. Lightweight AR Reranking** | Load a **tiny AR scorer** (GPT‑2‑small, 124 M) in fp16. For each set of N candidates compute the **average negative log‑likelihood (NLL)** of the full sequence (including EOS). Pick the candidate with lowest NLL as the “sampling + reranking” output. <br>• Scorer runs on CPU (single core) – negligible GPU load; we overlap scoring with GPU kernel launches via CUDA streams to hide latency. | Using NLL rather than a learned ranker keeps the reranker truly lightweight and comparable across models. Overlapping with GPU work ensures the efficiency metric reflects realistic deployment. | CPU‑only; < 0.5 GPU‑hr equivalent. |
| **6. Quality Evaluation** | For each output compute three task‑specific scores: <br>• **HumanEval**: pass@1 and pass@10 (using the official harness). <br>• **GSM8K**: exact‑match accuracy. <br>• **SIQA/WinoGrande**: accuracy (average of the two). <br>Normalise each score to \[0,1\] across *all* conditions (model × step × N) and take the **unweighted mean** → **Quality** ∈ \[0,1\]. | Normalisation removes scale differences between tasks, allowing a single aggregated quality metric while preserving interpretability. | CPU‑side; < 1 hr. |
| **7. Efficiency Measurement** | Measure **wall‑clock latency per generated token** (including scorer overhead) using `torch.cuda.Event` timestamps averaged over all prompts and repetitions. <br>Convert latency to **Estimated Compute**:  `EstCompute = latency_per_token × peak_FP16_throughput (≈ 140 TFLOPs)`. <br>Define **Quality‑Efficiency Trade‑off (QET) = Quality / EstCompute**. | Expressing efficiency in FLOP‑equivalents makes the metric comparable across different step budgets and sample counts, and aligns with the “compute allocation” framing of the problem. | GPU‑side timing; negligible overhead. |
| **8. Analysis Pipeline** | 1. **Marginal QET gains**: <br> a. ΔQET_denoise = QET(high steps, N=1) − QET(low steps, N=1) (and similarly low→medium, medium→high). <br> b. ΔQET_sample = QET(medium steps, N=8) − QET(medium steps, N=1) (and 1→4, 4→8). <br>2. **Preservation‑Gain Relationship**: Compute Spearman’s ρ between each model’s PI (or individual CKA/SVCCA/probe) and the two sets of marginal gains (ΔQET_denoise, ΔQET_sample). <br>3. **Interaction Tests**: Fit a **linear mixed‑effects model** <br> QET ~ PI * step_budget * N + (1|model) + (1|prompt_difficulty) <br> to test whether the slope of PI differs across step budgets and N, and whether GPT‑2 vs. LLaMA families exhibit different intercepts/slopes (include family as a fixed effect). <br>4. **Bootstrap Uncertainty**: Resample prompts (with replacement) 1 000 times, recompute PI, QET, and correlations; report 95 % confidence intervals. <br>5. **Decision Rule Exploration**: Derive a simple threshold‑based policy: if PI > τ then allocate extra compute to sampling + reranking else to denoising; optimise τ on the held‑out prompt set (grid search) and report the resulting QET improvement over a uniform‑step baseline. | Mixed‑effects modelling accounts for repeated measures (same prompt across conditions) and lets us generalise beyond the three (now four) models. The bootstrap provides non‑parametric confidence intervals without assuming normality. The decision rule translates the scientific finding into a practitioner‑friendly guideline. | Analysis (CPU): ≈ 2 days. Bootstrap (1 000 × lightweight recompute): ≈ 6 GPU‑hrs (can be run on a single GPU overnight). |
| **9. Validation & Robustness Checks** | • **Ablation of representation metric**: repeat analysis using only CKA, only SVCCA, only probe to ensure conclusions are not metric‑specific. <br>• **Quantisation sanity check**: compare QET curves for full‑precision vs. 4‑bit DiffuLLaMA to verify that quantisation does not invert the observed trend. <br>• **Alternative scorer**: repeat reranking with a *tiny* distilled GPT‑2 (≈ 30 M) to confirm that scorer size does not drive results. | These checks increase validity and demonstrate that the observed relationship is rooted in representation preservation, not in artefacts of the measurement pipeline. | Additional ≈ 4 GPU‑hrs. |
| **10. Documentation & Dissemination** | Write a concise technical report (≈ 6 pages) + release all scripts, Docker image, and a **README** with one‑click reproduction commands. Deposit generated outputs and metrics on an open‑access Zenodo repository. | Ensures reproducibility and enables other teams to extend the analysis to newer DLMs (e.g., LLaDA‑8B, DiffuCoder‑7B) without re‑running the full GPU pipeline. | 3 days (writing) + 1 day (packaging). |

**Rationale**

The proposed method directly targets the core causal claim of the research problem: *does the degree to which a diffusion language model retains the autoregressive representations of its pretrained backbone predict whether extra inference compute is better spent on more denoising steps or on generating more candidates and reranking with a lightweight AR scorer?*  

1. **Multiple, orthogonal preservation metrics** (CKA, SVCCA, linear‑probe) reduce reliance on any single estimator and capture complementary aspects of representation similarity (global alignment, subspace overlap, predictive utility). Combining them into a Preservation Index yields a more reliable latent‑space similarity score that can be compared across model families and scales.

2. **Expanded model set** (adding a mid‑size GPT‑2 model) lets us test whether the preservation‑gain relationship holds within a family, not just across families, increasing the generality of any discovered rule.

3. **Finer granularity in candidate generation** (N = 2, 4, 8) and an explicit quantisation ablation give a clearer picture of marginal returns and ensure that observed trends are not artefacts of precision loss.

4. **Unified quality metric** (normalised, task‑agnostic) enables a clean computation of Quality‑Efficiency Trade‑off (QET), which directly mirrors the “quality per unit compute” notion in the problem statement.

5. **Efficiency expressed in FLOP‑equivalents** translates wall‑clock latency into a hardware‑agnostic compute budget, making the trade‑off comparable across different denoising‑step budgets and sample counts.

6. **Mixed‑effects modelling and interaction terms** formally test whether the slope linking preservation to QET gains changes with denoising budget or candidate count, and whether GPT‑2 vs. LLaMA families differ—exactly the interaction analysis called for in the rationale.

7. **Bootstrap confidence intervals** provide rigorous, distribution‑free uncertainty quantification, satisfying the rigor requirement.

8. **Decision‑rule extraction** turns the scientific insight into an actionable guideline for practitioners: measure a cheap preservation score (e.g., CKA on a small validation split) and, based on a pre‑computed threshold, decide whether to invest extra inference steps or extra samples with reranking. This aligns with the goal of offering a “principled, diagnostic tool rather than a new training method.”

9. **Fe adherence to constraints**: all steps are inference‑only, use a single RTX 6000 Pro Blackwell (activation checkpointing + optional 4‑bit quantisation keeps the largest model within 96 GB), and the estimated GPU‑hour consumption fits comfortably into the 10‑week window with three GPU‑enabled team members.

By integrating robust similarity measurement, systematic compute‑allocation experiments, and principled statistical analysis, the method delivers a clear, reproducible answer to the research question while remaining generalizable to future diffusion language models.
